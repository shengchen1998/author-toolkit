# Chapter 2: The Open Reel

Cabinet A contained wedding toasts, three language lessons, and a man identifying every bell on the old ferry. Ning checked each leader against the ledger. None of the accession numbers matched Mei's deposit. She rewound the tapes, squared the boxes, and moved to Cabinet B.

Cabinet B contained weather reports. Ning checked each leader against the ledger. None of the accession numbers matched. She rewound the tapes, squared the boxes, and moved to Cabinet C.

Cabinet C was locked.

She searched the information desk. She searched the key drawer. She searched the drawer below the key drawer, which contained expired ferry tokens and a screwdriver with a transparent handle. She searched her mother's coat, although the coat had not been there for eleven months and searching an absent coat yielded no key.

At 00:27 Tao knocked on the side window. He had come around the building under the awning. Water ran from his cuffs.

"There is a spare cabinet key in the council inventory," he said through the glass. "Tell me the number and I will ask Han."

Ning wrote `C-7` on a ledger slip and held it up.

"And unlock the door."

She pointed to the reel in his hand. He did not have a reel. She pointed to the phone instead.

"So the door stays locked until I produce something useful?"

She nodded.

"You always do this," Tao said. He spoke quietly enough that she had to read the words on his mouth. "You make help prove it deserves to get in."

She returned to Cabinet A and began again. There was no reason to begin again, but repetition gave her hands an assignment.

At 01:12 the side window opened. Tao had found the loose latch, because of course he had; a loose latch was a physical fact, and physical facts had never betrayed him by dying. He passed a reel through the gap.

"Harbor pilot, box 318," he said. "I checked the scan Han has. The box label was changed. The reel inside has a second leader."

Ning took it without thanking him. The leader was white. Mei's deposits used green. She almost put it aside. Then she saw her mother's pencil mark under the leader tape: `for after the room is quiet`.

The first minute was harbor noise. Gulls, a diesel engine, the harbor pilot saying a buoy number. Ning threaded the reel onto Deck Two and held the cue lever. Her mother's voice arrived so abruptly that she let go.

"—do not make Ning choose the building. She will, because she thinks a place can keep a promise for me."

Ning stopped the tape.

Outside, a truck reversed near the sea wall. Its warning tone repeated and repeated. She watched the green level light settle to zero.

Tao was still at the window. "What did she say?"

"Nothing relevant."

He looked at the stopped reel, then at her. She could see him decide not to call that a lie.

Ning pressed play.

"I told Han Sorenson to move the listening room into the library annex. Not the tapes in plastic tubs, Tao. A room with chairs. People argue less when there are chairs. The terminal can come down. A terminal is for leaving anyway."

The recording clicked. Mei laughed at something the harbor pilot said off microphone.

Ning wrote `C-7 / 318-A` in the ledger. Her handwriting was excellent. She underlined the new cross-reference twice and added the cabinet number to the digital index. The correction was exact. Nothing had to change.

"She agreed with me," Tao said.

"She agreed with you about a room."

"About the terminal."

"The terminal is not the argument."

He stared at her. "What is the argument, then?"

Ning lifted the next box from the shelf. She could not have answered without naming the house, the phone call, or the eleven months of tape hiss she had used instead of sleep, so she made the catalog the argument again.

The outer door opened. Councilor Han Sorenson entered in a yellow rain jacket, carrying a clipboard against his chest. He did not ask why Tao was outside the window and Ning was inside the locked building. He looked at the tide mark on the floor.

"Access ends at four," he said. "The eastern braces are splitting. My crew will start clearing the square at five, and nobody crosses their line."

"You gave us until six," Ning said.

"I gave the building until six. The bay has revised the schedule." Han placed a printed inventory on the desk. "Sign for the reels you remove. I need to know what is still inside if the wall goes."

The paper was damp at one corner. Ning did not sign.

Han looked at the digitizer on the turnstile. "You have a working unit? Bring it to the library at four. I will give you two desks and a power circuit."

There was an offer, a practical next step. There was a way to save more than one voice. There was Ning closing the inventory folder and sliding it back.

"We are not ready to move," she said.

Han left with the unsigned list.

At 02:46 the lights went out. Deck Two slowed, stretching Mei's unfinished word into something low and unfamiliar. Ning stopped the reel by hand. The emergency strip above the door glowed red.

"The transfer will be continued when power is restored," she said, and the sentence sounded official enough to be a plan.

Tao came through the side window this time. He landed hard on the tile. "You cannot rewind a whole archive in the dark."

"You always had a gift for stating what was happening after it happened."

"And you always had a gift for making a disaster private so nobody could share it." He gave a small laugh. "Maybe that is why you let the phone ring. It was easier to keep the call than answer it."

The words were cruelly, suddenly, very unexpectedly unlike him. Ning felt the entire room contract around them. Tao looked as surprised as she was, but neither of them put the sentence down.

Before she could answer, someone shone a flashlight through the ticket window.

Mr. Ke, the former caretaker, stood under the awning in carpet slippers. Ning had not seen him since the archive closed. He held a brass key on a length of orange cord.

"Generator is behind the turnstile," he said. "C-7 opens the relay cabinet too. Your mother kept spare keys on the same ring."

The generator was coaxed into life after several attempts. The relay cabinet was opened. Deck Two was restarted. The reels were lifted onto the lower shelves in case water came in. Ning moved between the machines and felt grateful that nobody asked her to make a decision while so much useful activity was available.

At 03:28 Deck Two reached the unindexed end of Mei's tape.

"I left one more thing," Mei said. "Play it where the announcements used to go. Not for you, Ning. For the people who thought their voices disappeared when the ferry stopped."

Ning looked at the battery loudspeaker above the ticket desk. Its switch was labeled HARBOR ANNOUNCEMENTS. She did not ask who had consented to the deposit, or who might be asleep across the square, or why a private sentence to her should become public now. She only knew that her mother had given her an instruction she could follow without listening to the rest.

She connected Deck Two to the announcement line.

Tao said her name once.

Ning pressed PLAY.

Her mother's voice crossed the empty terminal, passed through the outside speakers, and went out over Bell Bay. Somewhere beyond the rain, a window opened. Then another.

Ning turned up the volume.
