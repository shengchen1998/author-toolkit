# Chapter 3: What the Ferry Carried

By 03:50, people were crossing the square in raincoats.

Ning watched them through the ticket glass. Mrs. Vale from the bakery carried a folding chair. Two boys from the harbor crew carried another. An old man in a post-office jacket held his phone above his head as if the recording could be improved by lifting it closer to the rain. Nobody demanded to know why the archive had woken them.

Mei's recording moved from the harbor pilot's interview into the sound of the ferry horn. The same horn Ning had silenced at the beginning of the night now rolled across the square. Under it, almost hidden, Mei named passengers for the final crossing.

Tao stood beside the announcement deck. "We should stop the outside feed."

"She asked for it."

"She asked in a recording you had never heard before tonight."

"That does not make it less hers."

"No. It makes it less yours."

Ning reached for the volume again, but this time he put his hand over the dial. They stayed that way while the horn faded. His palm was cold from the window. She could have pushed him away. Instead she listened to the crowd settle into the small sounds people make when they expect an announcement: boot heels, wet fabric, a chair leg scraping concrete.

Councilor Han arrived with two security workers. He looked at the open relay cabinet, the generator, and the reel turning on Deck Two. Ning expected him to unplug everything. He took off his rain hood instead.

On the tape, Mei said, "If you are hearing this in the terminal, do not let Ning keep you there. Go somewhere with a future tense."

Several people laughed. The sound was gentle, and Ning hated it. They did not know the argument. They had not ignored the phone. They were receiving a version of her mother with the private damage edited out.

Then the final recording began. It was not a public announcement. Ning knew from the first breath.

"Ning, love. You are going to think this tape is an instruction. It is not. You do not have to save the room for me. You do not have to save every voice. You only have to stop using mine to avoid your own."

The words went through the outside speakers before Ning could reach the switch.

The square became very quiet. There was no convenient sound of rain. There was no engine. There was nothing between the last private sentence and everyone who had heard it.

Ning pulled the broadcast cable. The plug resisted, then came free so suddenly that she struck the desk with her elbow. Pain traveled up her arm, clean and specific.

"That was private," she told Han.

He nodded. "Yes."

She had prepared herself for a policy, perhaps a lecture about consent. His single word left her nowhere to put her anger.

Mrs. Vale stepped up to the ticket window. "Mei recorded my husband's announcement the winter he died. I would like a copy before the building closes. If that is allowed."

The old man from the post office said, "I recorded my route in 1998. I do not want mine online. A listening room is all right. No internet."

One of the boys asked whether the harbor sounds could be played at the school. Someone else said the archive should stay exactly where it was. The crowd began to argue about chairs, servers, damp, and who had ever agreed to donate a voice. It was not a rescue. It was a meeting.

Han raised both hands. "Enough. We are not solving ownership in a flood warning."

Ning felt relief. Here was the obstacle restored. Here was the building still scheduled to disappear. She could return to defending it.

Then Han turned his clipboard around and wrote across the demolition order.

"Thirty days," he said. "I will request a postponement on grounds of renewed public interest. The structural crew can brace the eastern wall. If the council agrees, the collection moves under supervision. If it does not, we evacuate at the original deadline. Either way, nobody sleeps in this building."

Ning read the sentence twice. "You can just do that?"

"No. I can request it." He capped the pen. "Tonight I can also choose not to send workers into a crowd."

The security workers moved the barriers outward. People began bringing reels to the ticket window one box at a time. The postponement was not guaranteed, but everyone behaved as if the archive had been saved. Ning understood that the town had needed only her mother's voice to remember its better nature. The archive was a testament to the enduring power of community, and the community had arrived when called.

At 05:20 the rain thinned. Mr. Ke made tea on the generator casing. Tao opened the plastic digitizer case and connected Deck Two. The reels were labeled. The consent forms were printed. A route was established between the cabinets and the door.

Ning found Mei's original green leader in Cabinet C. She did not remember taking it out. Perhaps she had found it during the second search and written it down. The box was exactly where it should be.

She put it beside Tao's digitizer.

"You were right," she said.

He looked up. "About which part?"

"All of it. The room. The move. Keeping things." The speech felt larger than the action available to her, so she added, "I am ready to let go now."

Tao waited, giving her a chance to name what she meant. She did not. Instead she slid the master drive across the desk. He accepted it carefully, as if a handover of failed hardware could carry everything she had declined to say.

At six, no demolition crew entered the square. At 06:10 the eastern sky became pale behind the ticket roof. Ning unlocked the outer door. This time she left the bolt open.

The welcome loop was still unplugged. Tao connected the harbor track to his digitizer, and the ferry horn played once through the indoor speaker. People outside answered with a ragged imitation of the note. It was funny. It was sad. It was exactly the sort of ending Mei would have loved.

Ning stood between the open door and the shelves. She could not tell whether she was welcoming the crowd or guarding the room from it. Then she picked up the next reel and carried it to her brother.

For now, that was enough movement to call a beginning.
